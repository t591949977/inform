📋 Buyer's Guide

🔹Outerwear that will conquer the streets this fall

When summer heat gives way to autumn chills and rain, it's time to carefully prepare your wardrobe for the seasonal change. The most essential and challenging task is choosing the right jacket or coat for fall. Beyond aesthetics, it should offer water resistance, thermal protection and more.
Let's go through the key categories — from classic coats to utilitarian jackets — and break down what to look for when choosing.
Key selection criteria:
• Weather protection: water-repellent treatments and windproof fabrics for rainy days.
• Thermal insulation: linings and fillings that retain warmth during temperature fluctuations.
• Fit and comfort: freedom of movement and the ability to create layered looks.
• Durability: hard-wearing materials and quality hardware.

👗 History and Culture

🔹 Paris Green: the fashion that made heads spin (literally)

In the 19th century, Europe was gripped by a genuine green fever. The shade "Paris Green" was so bright, vibrant and provocative that everyone wanted it — from duchesses to grisettes. Hats, gloves, evening gowns, umbrellas — everything was awash in this radiant color. There was only one problem: the dye was made from arsenic. Yes, the same poison used for rat poison. But ladies weren't thinking about chemistry — they were thinking about beauty.

At balls, however, strange things began to happen: young women began fainting one after another. At first, people blamed the stuffy rooms, corsets and excitement. But the cause turned out to be simpler and more terrifying: when in contact with heated skin, the fabric released toxic fumes. The girls weren't just losing consciousness — they were slowly poisoning themselves just to make their rivals green with envy. They say the most dedicated fashionistas even boasted: "My green is so potent, I'm already staggering after the first waltz!" Alas, smiles are misplaced here — many paid with their health. But now we know for sure: green is the color of hope, not chemical safety.

🔹 Chopines: shoes for those who don't want to walk

16th-century Venetian aristocrats knew drama. Their footwear — chopines — were wooden platforms up to half a meter high. Imagine: you put on stilts, throw a brocade dress over them, and step out into society. Except you can't walk. At all. Your ankle simply won't bend, your center of gravity shifts toward Mars, and every step threatens an epic fall.

That's why noble ladies were supported by two maids — one on each side. The procession looked less like a social appearance and more like transporting a fragile vase. Why such sacrifices? Because the higher the chopines, the richer the family. Some flaunted platforms that barely fit a foot — pure snobbery on the edge of circus performance. The irony is that palazzo floors were deliberately made slippery, forcing ladies to move even more slowly and majestically. Today we call it a "red carpet" — back then it was "survival at altitude."
🔹The pencil skirt: born of wind and rope

1908. Mrs. Hart O. Berg prepares for a historic flight with the Wright brothers. One problem: she's wearing a long skirt, and there's a propeller spinning nearby. The wind on the runway is so strong that the fabric flaps like a sail. To keep the lady from taking off before the plane, she's cleverly tied with a rope right at the ankles. The flight goes smoothly, the skirt stays in place, and the lady, back on the ground, minces toward the car in tiny steps — just like a doll.

Watching this spectacle is designer Paul Poiret. And something clicks in his mind: "This is genius! Narrow, restrained, seductive!" And so the prototype of the pencil skirt is born. Of course, the first models weren't as elastic, and ladies had to walk like Mrs. Berg — in tiny steps. But beauty, as we know, demands not only sacrifices but also small steps. Today we wear pencil skirts with pleasure, and thank goodness, without ropes.
🔹Coco Chanel and the wrinkled rebellion

When Chanel released a linen collection, critics clutched their heads: "It's wrinkled linen! Where's the elegance? Where's the ironing?" Newspapers ran headlines like "Laundry Nightmare" and "Paris in Pleats." But Chanel wouldn't be Chanel if she gave up. The next day, she appeared before the journalists, adjusted a non-existent fold on her sleeve, and with icy calm declared: "This is not wrinkles, mesdames. This is noble drape."

And that was it. The magic of her name and absolute confidence did its work. Within a week, linen became the top trend, and critics quickly changed their tune — or rather, pressed themselves. Wrinkled suddenly became a mark of aristocratic nonchalance, as if to say, I'm so wealthy I don't even bother with an iron. Incidentally, this is when the meme "it's not a bug, it's a feature" was born — long before the computer age.
🔹Jeans versus teachers and censors

1950s, USA. The film "Rebel Without a Cause" starring James Dean is released, and suddenly all teenagers realize: jeans aren't just pants. They're protest. Freedom. Rock-and-roll on your knees. Schools raise the alarm: ban them! Jeans are outlawed in educational institutions, supposedly corrupting students and moral standards.

And what happens? Denim sales skyrocket. The stricter the ban, the more desirable the fabric. Parents panic, teachers tear their hair out, and teenagers pull on their blue pants with even more relish. Prohibition created a cult — pure irony. Today everyone wears jeans: from senators to punks, but few remember they were once considered weapons of mass destruction against school discipline.
🔹 Crinoline mania: fire hazard with lace

The 19th century — the age of massive hoop skirts. Crinolines were enormous: some skirts had diameters of several meters. Women entered rooms sideways, got stuck in carriage doors, and took up two seats at the theater. But the real horror began when someone sneezed near the fireplace. Lightweight fabric would ignite instantly, like a match. A candle, a spark from the stove, even hot ash — and a lady would become a human torch.

Over several decades, thousands of women died or suffered severe burns from their own outfits. Fire brigades of the time knew crinolines by sight — and feared them more than fire itself. But fashion held on until the bitter end. Because nobody wanted to admit that beauty could be deadly. Only when husbands started setting skirts on fire as a joke (not out of malice, just to demonstrate the danger) did people finally think twice.
🔹 Lead-based white makeup and mouse eyebrows: a Rococo beauty blog

In the 18th century, aristocratic pallor was the height of chic. To achieve porcelain skin, ladies applied thick layers of lead white mixed with vinegar. The acrid smell, chemical burns, grayish skin — but at least it was fashionable! Except lead spared no one: poisoning, hair loss, facial ulcers. Ladies went as bald as billiard balls, and to hide the gaps, they glued on artificial eyebrows... made from mouse fur.

Yes indeed, small strips of gray fur on the forehead were considered the pinnacle of elegance. Imagine a society chat: "Marie, your eyebrows are looking rather shiny today" — "Oh, it's a new mouse, very rare." We laugh, but this was real drama. Beauty cost literally skin and hair. Thank goodness we now know: pallor is treated with vitamins, not lead.
🔹The sweatshirt: a sweaty revolution

1920s. Benjamin Russell watches his football-player son suffer through a wool sweater. The wool irritates his skin, he's overheating, and the boy scratches more than he trains. So Russell takes some soft cotton and sews the first loose, crewneck pullover — the prototype of the sweatshirt. His son is happy, the team is thrilled, and within a year all the football players are wearing cotton.

Then the sweatshirt moved from the field to universities, from there to the streets, and eventually to high fashion. Today we wear it with sneakers and blazers, never thinking we owe it all to one young athlete's perspiration. The irony of life: what was made for sweat became a symbol of comfort.
🔹 Heels: a male invention with a female ending

If you think high heels were invented by women to torture themselves and delight men — you're wrong. They were invented by Persian horsemen in the 9th century, and strictly for practical purposes: a heel securely locked the foot in the stirrup when an archer fired from horseback. All function, no coquetry.

But in Europe, high heels were seen and deemed "status." Aristocratic men began wearing them to appear taller and more majestic. Heels clicked across the parquet floors of Versailles, kings strutted in them. And then, as usual, women took over the trend — and by the 18th century, high heels had become exclusively feminine. So, ladies, when you complain about the discomfort of stilettos, remember: originally they were worn by men with sabers.

🔹 Footwear that will keep you warm this fall

When summer warmth gives way to autumn days, the question of footwear selection becomes especially relevant. On one hand, it's the season of rain and slush, demanding reliable protection from moisture. On the other, autumn offers warm, sunny days with the rustle of fallen leaves, when you crave lightness and elegance. And of course, even in bad weather, a woman should remain impeccable — in the office, at a business meeting or on a walk. Therefore, the autumn footwear wardrobe is built on a balance of practicality and style.
Important criteria when choosing autumn footwear

  • Water resistance: look for water-repellent treatments and membranes.
  • Sole: should be slip-resistant (TPR or rubber).
  • Materials: genuine leather or quality textiles with water-repellent treatment.
  • Heel: for daily wear, choose a stable heel or platform.
  • Comfort: an anatomical insole and good foot fixation are important.

🔹 Overview of 10 of the most influential and renowned brands setting standards in footwear design:

 

Christian Louboutin — a legend of evening and red-carpet fashion, famous for its red soles. Opinion: A symbol of classic glamour, seductive silhouettes and sky-high stilettos (So Kate and Pigalle models). Statement footwear that creates a flawless silhouette and evening look.

 

Manolo Blahnik — synonymous with refined classicism elevated to an art form, thanks to "Sex and the City." Opinion: The iconic Hangisi pumps with crystal buckles — the pinnacle of elegance. The brand is renowned for an incredibly comfortable fit among high-heeled shoes.

 

Jimmy Choo — a giant of premium footwear for red carpets and bridal looks. Opinion: The brand masterfully combines jewel-like embellishments, delicate straps and contemporary trends. An excellent choice for formal events and bold accents.

 

Bottega Veneta — the leading innovator in contemporary footwear in recent years. Opinion: They introduced the voluminous Intrecciato woven leather, square toes and padded shapes (Padded Sandals, Lug boots). Perfect for those seeking cutting-edge design.

 

Amina Muaddi — a phenomenon of the modern footwear industry with a recognizable architectural pyramid heel. Opinion: Heels and sandals with bright crystals and vinyl have become the ultimate must-have for the social media generation and fashionistas.

 

Maison Margiela — conceptual avant-garde that revolutionized footwear design. Opinion: Their famous Tabi model with a split toe is the most recognizable silhouette in niche design, reflecting a deep understanding of contemporary fashion.

Gianvito Rossi — a master of understated Italian minimalism and high quality. Opinion: The son of Sergio Rossi perfected the basic pump and transparent silicone sandals (Plexi). The choice for those who appreciate clean lines and comfort.

 

Tod's — an icon of Italian quality and Quiet Luxury style. Opinion: Their moccasins with rubber studs on the sole (Gommino) and leather loafers with a signature buckle are the gold standard of everyday basic footwear.

 

The Row — a leader in contemporary minimalism and restrained luxury from the Olsen sisters. Opinion: The brand's ballet flats, minimalist sandals and booties have become iconic thanks to impeccable materials, the absence of logos and maximum comfort.

 

Roger Vivier — the historic French house that invented the stiletto heel and the rectangular metal buckle. Opinion: Their elegant ballet flats, straight boots and shoes with a rectangular buckle are the epitome of Parisian chic and timeless elegance.
