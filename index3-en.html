📋 Buyer's Guide

🔹Can you wear knitwear in summer?

Summer has arrived — the season of lightness, sunshine and vibrant colors. It's time to refresh your wardrobe and stock up on styles that will keep you comfortable even on the hottest days. Summer clothing should not only be stylish but also breathable, practical and versatile. After all, summer is not just about beach holidays — it's also about city strolls, meetings with friends, business lunches on open terraces and romantic evenings. This season, natural fabrics, relaxed silhouettes and bold accents are especially relevant.


Additional criteria for choosing summer clothing:
• Breathable materials: cotton, linen, silk, viscose — allow the skin to breathe and quickly absorb moisture.
• Relaxed fit: ensures comfort in the heat and does not restrict movement.
• Versatility: items that can be mixed and matched for different occasions — from the beach to the office.
• Color and print: bright shades, pastel tones and bold floral prints are popular in summer.
• Easy care: choose fabrics that don't wrinkle and are easy to wash — this will save you time during the busy season.

👗 History and Culture

🔹 Lipstick to match your car: fashion on wheels

The 1920s — the era of jazz, automobiles and freedom. Revlon launches nail polishes and lipsticks in coordinated color palettes. It was a breakthrough: a woman could shine from head to toe. But marketers went further: ads urged women to match their lip color not just to their handbag or hat, but... to their car!

"Driving a red roadster? Then your lips should be fiery. Have a blue convertible? Go for plum!" — posters proclaimed. And women bought into it. Imagine: morning, a woman applies lipstick matching her car, gets behind the wheel and feels at one with the engine. It sounds wild, but that's how the culture of the total look was born. Today it seems like charming vintage, but back then it was the boldest trend of the decade.

🔹 Fastenings for servants and masters

Have you ever noticed that buttons on men's shirts are on the right, while on women's they're on the left? It's not a tailor's mistake but a centuries-old tradition. It's simple: in wealthy families, ladies were dressed by maids. It was easier for them to button with their left hand when facing their mistress. Men, on the other hand, dressed themselves — so the buttons are on the right, for the right hand.

The irony is that we still follow this rule, even though women have been perfectly capable of buttoning their own clothes for over a hundred years. But tradition is stronger than logic. Now, when you struggle with the left-side buttons on your favorite blouse, just tell yourself: "It's not inconvenient — it's historical context."

🔹 The beard tax: when facial hair became a luxury item

Imagine: you wake up in the morning, look in the mirror and think — to shave or not to shave? And suddenly you realize that the size of your wallet depends on that decision. That's exactly how the subjects of Peter I in Russia and Henry VIII in England felt. These two monarchs, unknowingly, became the godfathers of the strangest tax in history: the beard tax.

In Russia, Peter the Great, obsessed with European fashion, outlawed beards for everyone except clergy and peasants. Want to keep your facial hair? Pay up! The amount depended on your status: wealthy merchants paid a fortune, the poor paid pennies. And to make sure no one evaded the tax, special "beard tokens" — metal badges confirming payment — were displayed at city entrances. Without one, you could be fined on the spot, or even have your beard forcibly removed (just kidding, but it certainly rattled nerves).

England's King Henry VIII, a lover of wives and luxury, also decided to tax bearded men. His motivation was simpler: fill the treasury, because wars and banquets were expensive. The irony? Henry himself wore a beard! He was essentially charging his subjects for the right to look like him. The beard became a marker: either you're rich and pay, or you're poor and go as smooth as a billiard ball. Today we laugh, but back then it was a serious social signal — literally "shaved means poor."

🔹 The hobble skirt: a step toward the emergency room

The 1910s. Women, inspired by progress and freedom, suddenly decided that walking with a normal stride was vulgar. Enter the "hobble skirt." It tapered so drastically at the ankles that a regular step became physically impossible. Women moved in tiny, shuffling steps, like chickens on ice. It was both endearing and tragic to behold.

Now imagine: a lady in such a skirt steps off a tram, trips on the curb — and falls like a statue. Her hands are full with a handbag and parasol, her legs are bound by fabric, and there's nowhere to steady herself. Ankle injuries, dislocations, bruises — it became a real epidemic. Doctors raised the alarm, and fashionistas paid for beauty not only with money but also with plaster casts. The irony is that this skirt was designed by Paul Poiret, who was inspired by a rope tied around an aviator's legs. The idea was good, but the execution was cruel. Women wore hobble skirts for only a short time — crossing the street was simply too dangerous. But it entered history as a symbol of how fashion can literally strip us of the ability to step forward.

🔹 Macaronis — not a dish, but a lifestyle

When you hear the word "macaroni," you think of a delicious Italian dish, right? But in 18th-century England, a macaroni was the trendiest guy in town. A subculture of young dandies who wore Italian clothes, enormous powdered wigs, tiny hats (barely perched on those wigs) and tight knee-length breeches. They spoke with Italian accents, ate macaroni (food, after all!) and behaved with maximum eccentricity.

These guys were a parody of themselves — they were mocked in newspapers and caricatures. But they didn't give up. They inspired the famous line from the American song "Yankee Doodle": "stuck a feather in his hat and called it macaroni." In other words, an American visiting London thought that sticking a feather in his hat made him a sophisticated European fashionista. In reality, macaronis were far more ridiculous: flowing shoulder-length curls, padded calves (yes, they stuffed their breeches with padding for volume!), and they moved with incredible self-importance. So the next time you eat pasta, remember: somewhere in the 18th century, macaronis with wigs and feathers were strutting around.

🔹 Mouse-skin beauty marks: moles with a twist

The 18th century — the era of lace, corsets and... pimples. No one was safe from smallpox, and facial scars and sores were considered a terrible faux pas. What to do? Enter beauty marks — tiny artificial moles stuck onto the face. At first, they were cut from velvet or taffeta. Then things took a surreal turn: beauty marks began to be made from... mouse skin. Yes, gray, soft and surprisingly realistic!

Each mark had its own meaning. A circle near the lips — coquettishness; a triangle on the cheek — readiness for intrigue; a star at the temple — membership in a secret society (or just a desire to impress). Ladies would apply five or six, creating veritable maps on their faces. But imagine: summer heat, resin-based glue melting, the mouse-skin mark sliding toward the nose... This wasn't chic — it was survival! And when a gentleman kissed a hand and suddenly tasted mouse fur — that was true romance. The irony is that many modern beauty bloggers don't know that their beloved concealers and patches are just the contemporary version of mouse-skin moles.

🔹 Chopines: mud conquered, but walking — not so much

Venetians have always been practical. In the 15th–17th centuries, streets were muddy, so ladies wore high wooden platforms — chopines — to keep their luxurious hems clean. At first, these were practical 10–15 cm "clogs." But then it became a competition: who could go higher! Platforms grew to 50 cm — about the height of a three-year-old child.

Now putting on chopines wasn't just getting dressed — it was an alpine feat. The foot was locked in the arch, the leg couldn't bend, and the center of gravity shifted so dramatically that a slight breeze could topple a lady like a bowling pin. So two maids on each side were essential accessories. They supported their mistress as she moved majestically (read: barely) through the palazzo. When she fell, she could break a leg, the platform, and step on a maid's foot. Why such sacrifices? To keep the hem clean! In the end: the dress was spotless, but the wearer was like a circus performer on stilts. Mud was conquered, but walking became a risky quest.

🔹 Codpieces as a universal safe

The 16th century. Men wore tight hose, and suddenly a special flap appeared on the front — the codpiece. Initially, it served a purely utilitarian purpose: quick access. But then the codpiece began to... grow. And grow. It became a full-blown construction of fabric stuffed with cotton, embellished with lace and even buttons.

And here's the funny part: men quickly realized that this "pouch" was a great place to store things! Inside codpieces, they kept coins, handkerchiefs, keys, and sometimes even a piece of cheese or an apple (just kidding, but entirely plausible). Imagine a dignified courtier at a ball, pulling a handkerchief from his pants to wipe his brow, and a few coins tumbling out. Or dancing with coins jingling in there. The richer the man, the more elaborate his codpiece — it was a status symbol, much like a $100,000 watch today. And when short jackets came into fashion, the codpiece peeked out from underneath like a third eye. Fashion was, well, very... expressive.

🔹Wigs with mousetraps: hairstyles with company

The late 18th century. Women's wigs were no longer just hairstyles — they were architectural structures up to a meter high. They were built to last for weeks or even a month. The construction included a wire frame, tow, horsehair, flour for powder, and pomade made from animal fat. And in this warm, nourishing environment, anything could breed! Lice, of course — they felt like they were at a resort. But the main horror was mice. Rodents attracted by the smell of fat would settle in the wig, gnaw the hair and build nests.

What did ladies do? They slept with metal cages on their heads, resembling birdcages. They were suspended from the ceiling to keep mice from running across their faces. Imagine: night, an aristocrat in a luxurious bed, and suspended above her on a string is a cage containing her sleeping wig. Funny? Yes. Terrifying? Absolutely. Husbands were afraid to approach their wives because a mouse's tail sometimes poked out of the hairdo. At balls, it was customary to tap the wig with a fan — to check if anyone was hiding inside. Now that was true extreme style.
🔹 Flour sack dresses: fashion as survival

The Great Depression in the US — a time when even flour was a luxury. But American women didn't give up: they started making dresses and shirts from cotton sacks used for flour and animal feed. The fabric was coarse but free. And what did manufacturers do? They noticed and began producing sacks with bright floral prints, lace trim, and even instructions: "Cut along this line to make a dress." It wasn't just marketing — it was solidarity.

Women held impromptu fashion shows in villages, where everyone wore "flour mill" outfits. The irony is that these dresses became symbols of solidarity and resilience. When a neighbor said, "Oh, what a lovely dress!" the reply was, "Thanks, it's a new batch of flour — very successful." Today, vintage collectors pay crazy money for such garments, unaware they were once worn in kitchens covered in flour. Fashion born from poverty became fashion born from nostalgia.

🔹 Summer sports: gear for outdoor activities and workouts

Summer has arrived — along with long-awaited trips to nature, outdoor workouts and active recreation. In the warm season, it's especially important to choose the right sportswear that not only provides comfort and freedom of movement but also looks stylish. Technical fabrics, thoughtful cuts and functionality are the key criteria when choosing summer sportswear.

Running in the park, yoga on the grass, tennis on the court or just a walk around the city — there's an ideal outfit for every type of activity.

 

Additional criteria for choosing sportswear:

• Moisture-wicking fabrics: draw sweat away from the skin, keeping you dry during workouts.
• Elasticity: materials should stretch well and not restrict movement.
• Flat seams: prevent chafing and irritation during high-impact activities.
• Reflective elements: ensure safety when running in low light.
• UV protection: protects the skin from harmful ultraviolet radiation outdoors.
