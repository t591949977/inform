🔹 Were eyes painted in ancient times?

Yes, and it was a widespread phenomenon. Eye makeup was particularly common in Ancient Egypt, where it was applied by men, women, and even pharaohs.

However, the reasons were far from modern beauty standards:

  1. Protection against infections. The paints contained lead, which upon contact with skin produced substances that killed bacteria and protected the eyes from inflammation.

  2. Protection from the sun. Dark eyeliner reduced the blinding effect of bright desert light.

  3. Religious significance. The lines around the eyes symbolized the eye of the god Horus and served as a talisman against evil forces.

In Mesopotamia, eye makeup was a sign of nobility, while in Ancient Greece it was considered the domain of foreigners. But in any case, it was not just a cosmetic practice, but a practical and sacred necessity.

🔹 Why Napoleon Bonaparte ordered buttons sewn onto the sleeves of soldiers' uniforms

There is an interesting legend linking the appearance of buttons on the sleeves of men's jackets to the name of Napoleon Bonaparte. According to this story, the emperor noticed during a troop inspection that the sleeves of his soldiers' uniforms looked indecent — they were dirty and soiled. The reason was mundane: the soldiers, many from peasant families, were used to wiping their noses and mouths with their sleeves instead of handkerchiefs.

To break the soldiers of this unhygienic habit, Napoleon ordered metal buttons to be sewn onto the sleeves of their uniforms. Now wiping one's nose with a sleeve became extremely uncomfortable and even painful — the buttons scratched the face, and mucus was not absorbed by the fabric, remaining on the metal. Over time, the soldiers broke the habit.

However, despite the popularity of this version, historians consider it more of an appealing legend than historical truth. Similar decorative elements on sleeves appeared as early as the 17th century — long before Napoleon. And the widespread use of metal buttons on military uniforms began only after 1784.

Nevertheless, this legend has firmly entered popular culture, and over time the buttons on sleeves migrated to civilian clothing, becoming a decorative element of men's jackets that no longer serves its original purpose.

🔹 Which country and in which year held the world's first official Fashion Week?

The world's first official Fashion Week took place in New York in 1943 and was called "Press Week."

It was organized by Eleanor Lambert — an influential New York publicist and press director of the New York Institute of Clothing.

Why then?

The reason was World War II. Editors of leading fashion magazines like Vogue and Harper's Bazaar could not travel to occupied Paris, which was the undisputed fashion capital at the time. To keep the American public from missing out on the season's novelties and, more importantly, to boost their own industry, Lambert organized press shows featuring the work of American designers.

This event became a triumph: American fashion finally gained worldwide recognition, and magazines that had previously been filled with French designs increasingly printed features on local designers.

From about the 1950s, "Press Week" officially became known as New York Fashion Week.

🔹Why did shoe toe length (poulaines) indicate nobility in the Middle Ages?

In the 14th and 15th centuries, Europe was gripped by an amazing fashion: men wore shoes with excessively long and pointed toes, which were called poulaines or Krakow shoes. The longer the toe, the higher the status of the owner — the wealthiest aristocrats ordered models with toes over 10–12 centimeters, and some specimens reached 45 centimeters.

The secret of this fashion was simple: it served as a demonstration of wealth and an idle lifestyle. In such shoes, it was impossible to move quickly or perform physical labor — they were the medieval equivalent of an expensive sports car or luxury watch. A person wearing poulaines showed everyone that they did not work with their hands and could afford to walk in deliberately impractical shoes.

The fashion became so extreme that authorities began to intervene. In 1463, King Edward IV of England passed a law prohibiting most of the population from wearing shoes with toes longer than two inches (about 5 cm). Those who violated the rules were fined, and shoemakers who produced overly long poulaines were banned from working in London. In Paris, a similar ban had been introduced earlier — in 1368.

An interesting detail: there is a version that poulaines also had erotic overtones. The shoes were made low at the ankle to emphasize colorful stockings and the man's legs, and some contemporaries associated toe length with virility. Because of this "indecency," the church called poulaines "Satan's claws."

By the end of the 15th century, the fashion for poulaines faded, giving way to wide square-toed shoes, but the history of this extravagant footwear remains one of the most striking examples of how clothing can serve as a language of status.

📅 About the Future of the Industry

🔹Is fashion a form of personal freedom or an instrument of social control and marketing?

Fashion is a paradoxical phenomenon. On one hand, it gives us freedom of self-expression. On the other, it subtly controls us, dictating what is "current" and what is "hopelessly outdated." So what is it really?

Fashion as freedom.

By choosing our clothes, we tell the world about ourselves. Style becomes a business card: through color, cut and accessories, we convey our mood, social status, belonging to a subculture, or simply declare our individuality.

Fashion gives us the right to experiment. Today you can wear a floor-length dress, tomorrow a leather jacket with combat boots, and the day after an elegant suit. And all of it is correct in its own way. In the modern world, there are almost no prohibitions: oversized coexists with fitted silhouettes, and bright prints with minimalism.

Fashion as an instrument of control and marketing.

But there is another side. The fashion industry is a huge business that thrives on constant trend changes. Every season we are told: "Your coat is no longer in style," "This color is outdated," "You need to urgently update your wardrobe." That's how marketing works — by creating artificial dissatisfaction.

Moreover, fashion has historically been an instrument of social control. Remember:

In the Middle Ages, shoe toe length indicated nobility.

Strict dress codes in offices and educational institutions still standardize appearance.

In some cultures, there are still rigid boundaries — how women and how men should dress.

Social media adds a new level of pressure: we compare ourselves to bloggers and influencers, forgetting that behind the perfect picture are filters, Photoshop and commercial contracts.

So where is the truth?

Fashion is an ambivalent instrument. It becomes freedom when you choose consciously — not blindly following trends, but using them as a palette for your style. Fashion becomes control when you follow it mindlessly, afraid of falling out of the "mainstream."

The answer is simple: fashion is just a canvas. And the artist is you. Only you decide what to wear and why. If the choice comes from the heart, not from the fear of being "unfashionable," you are on the right path.

🔹Have micro-trends died under the pressure of conscious consumption and the "forever wardrobe" concept?

There is no definitive "yes" or "no." Micro-trends have not died, but their influence is noticeably weakening. The endless chase for novelty is being replaced by another philosophy — conscious consumption and a "forever wardrobe."

Signs of the decline of micro-trends
In recent years, the fashion industry has been undergoing a tectonic shift. Consumers are tired of the endless rotation of micro-aesthetics like "clean girl," "mob wife," or "coastal cowboy" — each required new shopping and a new stylistic identity.

According to a report by the British resale platform Depop, based on the behavior of 43.5 million users, Generation Z is no longer constantly refreshing their wardrobe in favor of conscious choices. 78% of respondents often repeat the same looks, and 61% dress to feel confident, not to conform to a trend.

The capsule wardrobe market, valued at $1.3 billion in 2023, could grow to $2.6 billion by 2030. This is direct evidence of the growing popularity of the "less is more" concept.

What is replacing them?
Instead of short-lived micro-trends, several sustainable directions are emerging:

Normcore and "boring fashion." In 2025, this minimalist style is experiencing a rebirth. According to Edited, sales of basic items in neutral tones grew by 13% in the first quarter of 2025. On TikTok, the hashtag #normcore has over 140 million views. Consumers are choosing simple, recognizable silhouettes that can be worn for years.

Frugal chic. The term was coined by British blogger Mia McGrad, whose videos on a thrifty approach to wardrobes have garnered over two million views. The philosophy is simple: not saving for the sake of saving, but a conscious choice of items that are truly needed and will last a long time. According to PwC, Gen Z and millennials reduced their clothing spending by 13% in 2025 — and the trend is intensifying.

The modern uniform. More than half of young people find repeating the same looks liberating. Instead of constantly displaying novelty, they choose stability and recognizability.

What about micro-trends?
They haven't completely disappeared. Due to TikTok and Instagram algorithms, new aesthetics still appear almost every week. But attitudes towards them have changed.

Many consumers no longer follow trends mindlessly. Studies show that people are increasingly staying true to their own style — and behind this is a conscious exit from the consumer race, not just price pressure.

Moreover, even micro-trends have their paradoxes. The so-called "frugal chic" itself becomes a trend — with its own canon of "eternal" items (cashmere sweater, white shirt, leather baguette bag). Essentially, it is not a rejection of consumption but an upgrade: a transition from quantity to premium quality.

Conclusion.
Micro-trends have not died, but their era of absolute dominance is ending. Conscious consumption and the "forever wardrobe" concept are becoming the new norm — especially for the younger generation, which chooses confidence in constancy rather than novelty. Fashion is slowing down to say something more substantive.

🔹Can modern street fashion be called a full reflection of the era, or is it a crisis of ideas?

The answer to this question is paradoxical. Street fashion (hoodies, sneakers) today is both the brightest reflection of our time and evidence of a deep crisis of ideas in the fashion industry.

Reflection of the era: the voice of the streets and the demand for freedom
Modern street fashion was born from subcultures — skateboarding, hip-hop, graffiti, punk — as a form of protest and a way to make a statement. Today it has become a global language of self-expression. Hoodies and sneakers are not just clothing, but "wearable culture," a way to tell about yourself without opening your mouth.

The main achievement of street style is that it has brought fashion back to people's real needs. Comfort, practicality and functionality became more important than pomp. It is the street, not the runway, that now dictates trends — this is the direct opposite of the system that existed for decades. In this sense, street fashion is the ideal mirror of our era with its desire for freedom, authenticity and rejection of rigid rules. According to studies, more than 60% of young consumers choose brands that reflect cultural authenticity.

Crisis of ideas: when rebellion becomes business
However, it is precisely where the rebellion ends and big money begins that the crisis sets in.

In 2017, Louis Vuitton merged with Supreme — this moment became a symbol of how street fashion had finally entered the luxury system. The industry, built on engineering scarcity and status through logos, began to generate hundreds of billions of dollars. The real culture was diluted.

"Streetwear is dead" — these words of Virgil Abloh, one of the main architects of street fashion, now sound prophetic. He meant not the death of business, but the death of street fashion as a clearly defined cultural and subcultural phenomenon.

In the pursuit of scaling, the industry lost the most valuable thing — connection with the community. "Status is now defined by wearing brands that people don't know about. Being part of a community that is not widely known — that's the new engine of taste," notes Kelly Acheampong, founder of the Undiscovered platform. Fashion has become "homogeneous," as if everyone is copying each other.

Not death, but rebirth
However, modern street style has not died — it is being reborn into something new.

Micro-brands and "quiet" collaborations with niche communities are replacing loud and expensive collaborations. For example, Supreme's recent collaboration with the underground group SALEM shows that culture can exist without billion-dollar publicity.

Authenticity, connection, social message — this is becoming the new currency. Instead of logos and "hype" — meaning and responsibility. This is no longer just a "crisis of ideas," it is an attempt to return street culture to its original meaning.

Conclusion
It can be said that modern street fashion is simultaneously:

A full reflection of the era, as it answers the main demand of the time — to be oneself, free and comfortable.

A crisis of ideas that came at the moment when culture became a tool for making money, and its "code" ceased to be understandable.

The truth is that street fashion right now is a battlefield between the spirit of freedom and market mechanisms. Hoodies and sneakers will be with us for a long time, but the question is what meaning they will carry tomorrow.

🔹What is the difference between blindly following trends and developing an authentic personal style?

At first glance, both look fashionable. But on closer inspection, the difference between them is like between a copy and the original.

Blind trend following.
Who is it:

A person who completely updates their wardrobe every six months because bloggers or magazines say so.

Their behavior: Buys everything that's "trendy," even if it doesn't suit them.

Throws away or hides last season's clothes (they're no longer "relevant").

Feels anxious if they haven't managed to buy "that" bag or those sneakers.

Dresses to please others, not themselves.

Result: They look like everyone else. They have no face, only the mask of the current season. Their wardrobe is a collection of other people's ideas, bought with their own money. Such people often feel emptiness because clothes don't reflect their personality.

Authentic personal style.
Who is it: A person who knows themselves — their colors, silhouettes, body shape and character.

Their behavior: Studies trends, but takes from them only what suits them.

Invests in quality basics that will last for years.

Experiments consciously, not for fear of "falling out of fashion."

Dresses for themselves — and enjoys the process.

Result: They have a signature. They are recognizable in a crowd. They are not afraid to look different, because their confidence comes from within. Clothes work for them, not they for clothes.

🔹How will artificial intelligence change the work of designers and trend forecasting?

Artificial intelligence does not replace designers, but radically changes their role. From executors, they become directors and curators.

Previously, concept creation took days: references, sketches, color selection. Now neural networks generate hundreds of options in minutes. The designer's task is not to draw, but to precisely formulate the request, select the best and refine the details. AI takes over routine, leaving the person time for meaning.

Even more radical changes are in trend forecasting. Previously it was an art based on intuition. Now algorithms scan millions of images on social media and from shows in real time. They notice micro-changes — shifts in shade or shape — long before the human eye sees them. Trends are now predicted not a year ahead, but by weeks. Brands can react instantly.

In addition, AI makes design personalized: instead of one average collection, modules are created that adapt to the individual user.

But there is also a risk: AI learns from the past. If you feed it only successful examples, it will produce safe and similar solutions. Humans are needed for irrationality — the ability to break patterns and create the unexpected.

The conclusion is simple: it's not those who draw better who win, but those who ask better questions. Designers will stop fighting for time to sketch and start fighting for meaning. And that is much more interesting.
